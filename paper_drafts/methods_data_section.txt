--- 3. Data and Study Area ---

3.1  Crop and Market Scope

This study covers three essential horticultural commodities — tomato
(Solanum lycopersicum), onion (Allium cepa), and potato (Solanum
tuberosum) — collectively known as TOP crops, which together account
for approximately 30% of India's total vegetable production by
volume and exhibit characteristically high price volatility due to
their perishable nature, limited cold-chain penetration, and
concentration of production in a small number of states (Kumar et
al., 2021). The analysis period spans ISO calendar weeks from 2
January 2017 to 27 July 2026 (approximately 500 weeks), capturing
three complete agricultural price cycles, the COVID-19 disruption
period (March-June 2020), and two of the largest documented price
crises in the study window: the June-September 2023 tomato spike
and crash, and the August 2023-January 2024 onion export-restriction
episode (see Section X, Crisis Backtesting).

3.2  Primary Data Source: Agmarknet Weekly Price Panel

Weekly wholesale price and arrivals data were obtained from the
Agricultural Marketing Information Network (Agmarknet), the
Government of India's real-time electronic market reporting platform
that covers over 7,000 Agricultural Produce Market Committee (APMC)
mandis across all states and union territories. Raw transaction-level
data were downloaded for all three crops and refreshed periodically
throughout the study (most recently through late July 2026);
onion's raw source required a separate merge step reconciling the
Agmarknet portal's "Daily Price Report" and "Daily Arrival Report"
exports into the same market-identifier schema as the primary
scraper-derived file, since onion's original data source is not
topped up on the same cadence as tomato and potato.

Price records were aggregated to the market-week level using the
arrivals-weighted mean of daily modal prices:

    P_{m,t} = Sigma_d (p_{m,d} x q_{m,d}) / Sigma_d q_{m,d}

where p_{m,d} is the modal price (Rs/quintal) and q_{m,d} is the
arrivals volume (tonnes) on day d of ISO week t for market m.
Weekly arrivals were summed across all days within each ISO week.
Markets reporting zero or negative arrivals in a given week were
excluded from that week's computation but retained in the panel.

Before any coverage-based filtering, the raw panel contained 1,725
tomato markets, 1,580 onion markets, and 87 potato markets. The
markedly smaller and more geographically concentrated potato sample
reflects the crop's production geography (Section 3.3 details the
two states retained after filtering) and the limited number of
regulated APMCs actively reporting potato trade throughout the
study period.

3.3  Market Selection Criterion: Temporal Coverage Threshold

A key challenge in any long-run panel study of Indian agricultural
markets is incomplete temporal coverage: APMC reporting frequency
varies substantially by state, season, and crop, resulting in many
markets with long gaps or very sparse participation in the Agmarknet
system. Including markets with low coverage inflates the panel but
introduces structural missingness that degrades feature computation
(particularly lag and rolling-window features) and produces
unreliable model estimates.

For each market m and crop c, we computed a real (non-imputed)
temporal coverage index over that market's own reporting span --
from its first observed week through the end of the study period --
rather than a fixed calendar-wide denominator, so that markets
which began reporting after 2017 are not structurally penalised for
weeks before they existed in the system:

    TC_{m,c} = W_real_{m,c} / W_span_{m,c} x 100

where W_real_{m,c} is the number of ISO weeks for which market m
reported a real (non-imputed) price observation, and W_span_{m,c} is
the number of ISO weeks between market m's first reported week and
the end of the study period.

Markets were retained in the analytical panel only if TC_{m,c} >=
70% (potato markets additionally had to satisfy a balanced-panel
pre-filter of real data present in at least 8 of the study's 9-10
calendar years before the coverage test was applied). The threshold
was set at 70%, revised down the same day from an initial 80%: a
market-level Diebold-Mariano robustness check (Section 5.X, Script
18b) run at the 80% threshold found the onion h=1-week headline
result resting on only 34 of 189 statistically significant markets;
the 70% threshold roughly triples that supporting sample while still
excluding the large majority of sporadically reporting mandis.

A grid-construction error was found and corrected on 1 August 2026:
the weekly grid each market's real-coverage ratio TC_{m,c} was
computed over had originally been built from one fixed calendar
START_DATE common to every market, rather than starting at each
market's own W_span_{m,c} as the formula above specifies -- so a
market that only began reporting to Agmarknet partway through the
study window was silently scored as "missing" for every week before
it existed in the system, understating its real coverage for reasons
unrelated to its actual data quality. Correcting the grid to start
at each market's own first real observation is what the coverage
index was always intended to measure; at this study's 9-year window
the correction is modest in relative terms but still substantial in
absolute market count, since a large cohort of markets across both
tomato and onion evidently began reporting, or began reporting
consistently, only from around 2024 onward.

Application of the (corrected) 70% threshold yields an analytical
panel of 834 tomato markets (22 states), 809 onion markets (25
states), and 82 potato markets (2 states) -- respectively 48.3%,
51.2%, and 94.3% of each crop's raw (unfiltered) market count --
containing 300,344, 172,682, and 40,988 market-week rows (Table 1).
Among selected markets, minimum real coverage was 70.0-71.6% by
construction and median coverage exceeded 92% for all three crops --
the newly-recovered markets are not thin/borderline additions
(median coverage among them remains 92-96%), simply markets that had
been unfairly excluded by the grid artefact rather than by genuinely
low data quality. Potato's two retained states, West Bengal and
Uttarakhand, reflect a deliberate relocation of the crop's three
agro-climatic satellite/climate zones (P1-P3) to real production
districts (Darjeeling, Diamond Harbour, and Dehradun respectively)
partway through the study, after an earlier zone assignment was
found to reference districts with no corresponding satellite/climate
coverage. Tomato and onion's dominant states are unchanged by the
grid-construction fix (tomato: Uttar Pradesh, Kerala, Punjab; onion:
Maharashtra, Kerala, Haryana), indicating the newly-recovered markets
are broadly distributed across the same states already represented
rather than concentrated in new geography.

3.4  Macro-Economic and Logistics Covariates (Layer 4)

Monthly macro-economic indicators were obtained from three sources
and aligned to the weekly panel by matching on (year, month):

(a) CMIE Prowess and CMIE Economic Outlook: agricultural credit
    outstanding (Rs crore), vegetable export and import values (USD
    million), crude oil Indian Basket price (USD/barrel), Index of
    Industrial Production for food processing (base 2011-12), and
    national agricultural wages (Rs/day).

(b) RBI Database on the Indian Economy (DBIE): repo rate (%),
    reverse repo rate (%), monthly average USD/INR exchange rate,
    and Wholesale Price Index for all commodities, food articles,
    and fruits and vegetables (base 2011-12).

(c) Petroleum Planning & Analysis Cell (PPAC): retail diesel price
    at Delhi (Rs/litre) and LPG price (Rs/14.2 kg cylinder), used as
    a proxy for transportation and cold-storage cost.

All three macro series were extended in place as new CMIE Economic
Outlook exports became available and now cover January 2017 through
mid-2026 (113-114 monthly observations per series). Monthly values
were broadcast to the weekly level by matching each ISO week to the
month containing its start date. Column-level mappings for each
extended series were validated against known overlapping historical
values before being trusted, surfacing two pre-existing mislabelling
quirks in the originally published CMIE series (agricultural-wage
and food-processing-IIP column headers) that were retained as-is for
continuity with the earlier data rather than silently corrected.

3.5  Satellite and Climate Data (Layers 2 & 3)

Sentinel-2 (10 m, Level-2A), MODIS, ERA5-Land, and CHIRPS data were
accessed via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) cloud platform for the
production districts corresponding to each crop's agro-climatic
zone assignment (Section 3.3). For each zone-week, the following
features were extracted: maximum, minimum, mean, and diurnal-range
temperature and a >=35C and >=38C heat-day count from ERA5-Land
hourly reanalysis (0.1 deg x 0.1 deg grid); total and maximum daily
rainfall and an excess-rainfall-day count from CHIRPS; Sentinel-2
NDVI, EVI, the valid-pixel (cloud-free) fraction, and the NDVI
anomaly relative to each zone's own historical weekly mean; and
MODIS NDVI, EVI, mean and maximum land-surface temperature, and a
>=35C land-surface-temperature-day fraction, providing a coarser but
cloud-resilient vegetation and thermal-stress signal alongside
Sentinel-2's higher-resolution but cloud-limited optical record.
Values with an implausible EVI magnitude were masked as cloud-
contamination artefacts before aggregation to ISO-week composites.
Cross-validation between the Sentinel-2 and MODIS vegetation indices
confirmed consistent seasonal patterns across both sensors for all
three crops' zones.

3.6  Infrastructure and Labour Covariates (Layer 5)

State-wise monthly agricultural wage rates were obtained from
Labour Bureau publications, distinct from and complementary to the
national-level CMIE wage series in Layer 4 (Section 3.4). Cold-
storage capacity (registered facilities and rated tonnage) and road
density were compiled by state from published government sources;
because these infrastructure measures change slowly, cold-storage
and road-density figures were treated as approximately static
within the study period and broadcast to all weeks of the relevant
years, in contrast to the weekly- or monthly-resolution series used
elsewhere in the pipeline.

3.7  Policy and Trade-Intervention Covariates (Layer 6)

A primary-source-verified event log was compiled for Government of
India export-policy and market-intervention actions affecting the
three crops: export bans, minimum export price (MEP) notifications,
export duty changes, buffer-stock procurement and release actions,
Market Intervention Scheme (MIS) revisions, and Operation Greens
transport/storage subsidy activations. Each event record cites its
originating notification, source agency, and (where available) a
direct government source URL, and is tagged with a verification
status distinguishing primary-source-confirmed events from
retrospectively reported ones. The event log was converted into five
weekly policy-state features per crop -- export-ban status, MEP
level (USD/tonne), export duty rate (%), a market-intervention flag,
and an Operation Greens activity flag -- by forward-filling each
event's effective date until superseded by a later event of the
same type. This layer is the input Section X (Crisis Backtesting)
identifies as the direct mechanism behind the model's medium- to
long-horizon forecasting advantage during the 2023-24 onion export-
restriction episode, since the model observes the same duty/MEP/ban
sequence as a feature rather than having to infer it indirectly from
price alone.

3.8  Data Consistency and Imputation

Unlike an earlier draft of this pipeline, missing trading weeks
within a market's reporting span are not left as structural gaps:
short and medium gaps are filled using a two-tier imputation
policy (linear interpolation for brief gaps, seasonal-median
imputation for longer or seasonally patterned gaps), with the
imputation method recorded per row (`imputed`, `imputed_method`
columns) so that any downstream analysis can distinguish real trade
observations from filled ones. Across the filtered panel, 460,557 of
514,014 market-week rows (89.6%) are real observations; 20,675 rows
(4.0%) were linearly interpolated, 7,587 rows (1.5%) were imputed via
seasonal median, and 25,195 rows (4.9%) remain unfilled where a gap
exceeded the imputation policy's tolerance. A separate, higher-
resolution real-coverage index (Section 3.3) governs which markets
are admitted to the panel at all; imputation governs how within-panel
gaps for admitted markets are handled. Notably, a market's single
most recent reporting week is disproportionately likely to be
imputed rather than real (40.9% of markets, as of the 1 August 2026
grid-construction fix and panel expansion -- down from 76.2%
previously, since the large cohort of markets recovered by that fix
had mostly onboarded recently and are disproportionately likely to
still be actively reporting), a recency-lag effect the modelling
pipeline (Section 4) accounts for by separating a market's last real
observed price from its possibly-imputed most recent panel row.

The COVID-19 lockdown period (March-June 2020) produced a visible
dip in reporting across all crops, most pronounced for tomato
markets in Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh; markets whose lockdown gap
exceeded the imputation policy's tolerance in that window but
retained sufficient coverage across the full study period passed
the 70% threshold as normal.
