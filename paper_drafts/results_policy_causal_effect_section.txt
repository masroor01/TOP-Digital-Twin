--- X. Why the Forecasting Model Cannot Recover a Policy-Effect
       Magnitude, and What a Dedicated Causal Design Finds Instead ---

X.1  Motivation

The formal stress-testing module (Section X, Script 30) applies
calibrated real-world scenarios -- an export ban, a minimum export
price (MEP) increase, an export duty increase -- to the M6 production
model and finds that its predicted price response to onion's three
export-policy levers is, after a monotonic sign constraint, suppressed
to approximately zero. This is not a defect to be engineered away: it
is the correct behaviour of a model facing an identification problem
rather than a modelling one. Export duty, MEP, and the outright export
ban activated together, in sequence, in a single historical episode
(19 August 2023 - 4 May 2024; Section X.2 of the Crisis Backtesting
section), so a single time-series model trained on that one episode
has no independent variation with which to separate their individual
effects. The safest fit under a monotonicity constraint -- itself
imposed because an earlier, unconstrained model occasionally predicted
a price *increase* from tighter export policy, an economically
untenable result -- is simply "no effect."

This section reports a dedicated causal-inference investigation of the
same question, conducted independently of the forecasting pipeline,
using Synthetic Difference-in-Differences (SDID; Arkhangelsky, Athey,
Hirshberg, Imbens & Wager, 2021) and its extensions. The investigation
proceeds through four stages, each motivated by a specific failure of
the one before it, and concludes with a structural explanation for why
the effect is not recoverable from this dataset by any of the designs
attempted -- a negative result that is itself methodologically
informative, not merely an absence of a positive one.

X.2  Stage 1: Single-Episode SDID (Script 31, Parts A-C)

The baseline design treats onion (block-averaged across all markets
with >=95% real weekly coverage over the estimation window) as the
treated unit and tomato+potato (same coverage bar) as the donor pool
from which synthetic-control unit weights and DID time weights are
fitted. The 2023-24 episode is split into an escalation sub-window
(duty/MEP only, 19 Aug-7 Dec 2023) and a postban sub-window (8 Dec
2023 onward), because a first pass on the full window found a
confounded, strongly positive ATT: the unseasonal-weather supply shock
that prompted the policy response continued worsening through the
escalation period independently of the duty, and only the outright
ban is followed by the genuine price crash (Section X.2 of the Crisis
Backtesting section).

The escalation-window estimate (ATT = +56.2%, jackknife 90% CI
[+52.3%, +60.1%], stable under leave-one-donor-out refits of the
top-15 donor markets) passes its cross-crop placebo check: tomato's
placebo ATT in the identical window is -30.7%, and potato's is +4.3%,
both clearly distinguishable from onion's. The postban estimate,
however, FAILS this same check: onion's postban ATT (+14.5%) is
statistically indistinguishable from the placebo ATTs computed for
tomato (+14.5%) and potato (+15.2%) in the same window (jackknife 90%
CIs overlap), indicating a shared cross-crop shock -- most plausibly
a common seasonal or macroeconomic movement -- dominates whatever
ban-specific effect exists, and the tomato/potato donor pool cannot
net it out. A within-onion design (treated = Nashik-belt export-hub
markets, donor = non-hub onion markets, sidestepping the cross-crop
confound entirely) was run as a second design; its real-2023 postban
estimate (+3.7%, 90% CI [+0.9%, +6.6%]) is small but nominally
distinguishable from zero -- however, its own placebo-in-time check
(the identical design re-run at fake 2021 treatment dates, when no
real export restriction existed) ALSO returns a statistically
non-zero estimate (-3.9%, 90% CI [-6.3%, -1.4%]), of opposite sign,
indicating the hub-vs-non-hub design itself carries a structural bias
unrelated to any real policy shock. Neither design's postban estimate
survives scrutiny.

X.3  Stage 2: Formal In-Space Placebo Permutation Test (Script 38)

The cross-crop placebo check above compares the treated ATT against
only two aggregated placebo units (tomato-average, potato-average).
To test the postban estimate against a proper null distribution, each
of the 214 individual tomato/potato donor markets qualifying for the
postban window was in turn treated as a placebo-treated unit, with
the SDID procedure refit against the remaining 213 markets as its own
donor pool (Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller, 2010, in-space placebo
design). The resulting null distribution is centred near zero (mean
-0.15 log-points) with a standard deviation of 0.096 log-points; the
real onion postban ATT (+0.136 log-points, +14.5%) is matched or
exceeded in absolute magnitude by 28 of the 214 placebo markets
(rank-based two-sided p = 0.131), failing to clear a conventional 10%
significance threshold. This confirms, with a substantially larger
and more rigorous null reference than the two-unit comparison in
Stage 1, that the postban effect is not distinguishable from the
ordinary market-to-market variation the same procedure produces when
no real treatment is present.

X.4  Stage 3: Multi-Episode Verification

A natural response to Stage 1-2's null result is that a single
treated episode provides no repeated-trial variation with which to
average out episode-specific confounds. Cross-referencing an
independent policy-event compilation against DGFT notification
records identified two further, primary-source-verified onion export
bans within the panel's existing 2017-2026 window: 29 September 2019
(DGFT Notification 21/2015-2020, lifted 15 March 2020) and 14
September 2020 (DGFT Notification 31/2015-2020, lifted 28 December
2020). A rainfall-anomaly check (CHIRPS, onion production zones,
matching the diagnostic already used to identify the 2023 episode's
confound) confirmed both additional episodes also coincide with real
weather shocks, but of the OPPOSITE character to 2023's: +46.2 and
+55.4 mm/week wetter than the ISO-week climatological baseline for
2019 and 2020 respectively, versus -23.9 mm/week (drier than normal)
for 2023. Three episodes whose confounds differ in sign offer a
genuine test that no single episode can: a price response that
consistently aligns at the same event-time offset across all three,
despite oppositely-signed weather anomalies, cannot be a pure weather
artefact.

A stacked event-study design was constructed accordingly (Script 39):
the identical SDID procedure (onion treated, tomato+potato donors,
coverage re-evaluated on each episode's own window) was independently
fit for each of the three episodes, and the resulting dynamic-ATT
trajectories (Section X.2's event-study decomposition, generalised
across episodes) were aligned by weeks-since-ban rather than calendar
date and stacked. The result IS consistent across all three episodes:
onion separates from a stacked tomato+potato placebo band across most
of the post-ban window, most strongly at weeks +20 to +26 (stacked
mean gap +33 to +71 percentage points), collapsing toward zero within
1-3 weeks of each episode's real ban-lifting date (15 Mar 2020, 28 Dec
2020, and 4 May 2024 respectively -- weeks +24, +15, and +21 post-ban
in event time). This is a striking, mechanistically coherent pattern.

Critically, however, the sign of the effect is the OPPOSITE of what a
successful ban would produce: onion prices run consistently HIGHER
than the synthetic control throughout the post-ban window across all
three episodes, not lower. Combined with the observation (Section X.2
of the Crisis Backtesting section; also confirmed directly for all
three episodes here) that in every case price continued rising for
several weeks AFTER the policy intervention before eventually
falling, the most parsimonious explanation is reverse causality
rather than a ban effect: each ban was announced BECAUSE onion prices
were already escalating faster than tomato or potato at that specific
moment, so a synthetic control built from tomato/potato prices will
mechanically show onion diverging upward around every real ban date,
independent of whether the ban itself has any causal effect at all.
Stacking additional episodes does not resolve this, because the
confound here is not weather (which does differ across episodes and
would be expected to partially cancel) but the endogenous TIMING of
the policy response itself, which is structurally the same mechanism
in all three episodes.

X.5  Stage 4: Generalisation Check Across Crops

A final check asked whether this endogeneity is specific to onion's
export-ban regime or a general property of how these interventions
are triggered. Two additional documented events -- tomato's July 2023
NAFED/NCCF procurement intervention and potato's June 2014 minimum
export price ($450/MT) -- were checked directly against the panel's
own price history in the weeks preceding each policy action. Tomato's
price rose from approximately Rs 1,300-1,400/quintal in April-May
2023 to Rs 7,557/quintal by 31 July 2023 (a >5-fold increase) before
the procurement intervention, which landed within one week of the
observed peak. Potato's price rose from Rs 760/quintal (early March
2014) to Rs 1,404/quintal (23 June 2014, three days before the MEP
notification) -- a near-doubling over roughly fifteen weeks. Every
documented price-policy intervention identified for any of the three
crops in this study was preceded by a substantial price movement in
the direction the policy was designed to counteract. This is not
incidental: these instruments (MEP, export duty, export bans, buffer
procurement) are, by design and stated government objective in every
case, reactive tools triggered by an already-visible price signal.
The reverse-causality problem identified for onion in Stage 3 is
therefore a general feature of the data-generating process for
agricultural price policy in this study period, not a limitation of
the onion-specific design.

X.6  Discussion and Implications

Four independent lines of evidence -- a single-episode cross-crop
placebo failure, a within-onion design whose own placebo-in-time
check fails, a formal permutation-based null-distribution test, and a
three-episode stacked design whose consistent signal is explicable by
reverse causality rather than treatment -- converge on the same
conclusion: the causal effect magnitude of India's onion export
restrictions is not identifiable from the available historical panel
using standard quasi-experimental designs, because treatment timing
is endogenous to the outcome by construction. This differs from, and
is a stronger claim than, "the effect is small" -- it is that no
design attempted here can distinguish a genuine effect of any size
from the confounding structure of when these policies are invoked.

This has two practical implications, one for the model's role as a
decision-support tool and one for policy design itself. First, the
production forecasting model's near-zero stress-test response to
export-policy scenarios (Section X, Script 30) should be understood
and presented as an honest reflection of a genuine identification
limit in the underlying data, not as evidence that export policy is
ineffective, nor as a defensible causal estimate of any specific
magnitude. Second, and more broadly, the finding that every
documented price-stabilisation intervention across all three crops in
this study was preceded by a substantial adverse price movement
indicates that current policy is structurally reactive rather than
anticipatory. This is precisely the gap the forecasting side of this
work is positioned to help close: a model demonstrated (Section X,
Crisis Backtesting) to out-perform naive persistence by a wide margin
at 13-26 week horizons during real historical crises offers a
mechanism for earlier intervention -- and, should such earlier,
less-confounded intervention occur in the future, a genuine
identification opportunity that the present, entirely reactive
historical record does not offer.
